The big one

Weight-loss drugs just moved the aging clocks

Image: Stephanie Arnett / MIT Technology Review

At the Aging Research & Drug Discovery meeting, Novo Nordisk and Eli Lilly put numbers on a question longevity people have been whispering about for two years: do GLP-1 drugs change biological age, not just waistlines? Both firms say yes. Novo reported that patients on semaglutide showed improved biological age across trials and tissues, roughly two to three years on average, and as much as four years on a clock built from heart proteins. Lilly saw the same direction with tirzepatide on epigenetic clocks. Horvath, who invented the first widely used aging clocks, called two years "a pretty strong effect" and floated the g-word: geroprotectors.

My read: this is the mass-market version of the knee of the curve. Yesterday's ER-100 story was three eyes and a safety board. This one is the best-selling drug class on earth, already in about one in nine American adults for weight loss, now arguing it bends the molecular clocks that mark aging itself. For Longevity Science News, that is not a side note. It is the plot. The catch sits in the population, not the press release: Harvard's Vadim Gladyshev, who helped Novo with the measurements, said it looks like an anti-aging drug in an unhealthy population, and nobody knows what it does in a healthy one.

The catch: clocks are not yet an FDA surrogate for aging. Muscle loss is real. ARPA-H has $38 million on a Texas study to test semaglutide in healthy people over sixty, which is the trial that will either baptize this claim or put it back in its box. Until then, anyone who posts "Ozempic reverses aging" without the caveat deserves a damn good flogging, and you can say so on camera.

By the numbers:

  • ~2–3 years biological-age improvement Novo cites across trials and tissues
  • Up to ~4 years on a heart-protein clock
  • 10,052 people in Novo's blood-draw / proteomic-clock analysis (half drug, half placebo)
  • 11% of U.S. adults now on a GLP-1 for weight loss, up from 3% in 2024 (Gallup)
  • $38M ARPA-H study testing anti-aging effects in healthy adults over 60

Sources: MIT Technology Review · Gallup · We Will Cure / FAST
