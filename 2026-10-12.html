The big one

Atomic Machines debuts its code-built switch today

Jeff Holden, founder and chief executive of Atomic Machines. Image: Atomic Machines via TNW

Atomic Machines' first product makes its public debut today at the Open Compute Project Global Summit in San Jose, which runs Oct 12–15. The company came out of six years in stealth on Oct 7 with $250 million raised. Its machine is the Matter Compiler, an "AI-native, all-digital manufacturing system that builds working machines directly from code." No molds, no masks, no hard tooling.

Founder Jeff Holden built Amazon Prime and was later Uber's first chief product officer. His line: "3D printing made shapes programmable… We're making machines programmable." The first product, PrimeSwitch, is a tiny relay for AI data centers moving to 800-volt DC power, where a fault does its damage in microseconds.

My read: this is the curve climbing out of the screen. Software got cheap decades ago; tiny hardware stayed stubborn, each new device class needing years of bespoke process work. Holden puts the AI inside the loop, measuring and correcting every step and running its own experiments on its own hardware. Gigafund's Luke Nosek calls it "the start of vibe manufacturing." If a new machine costs what a design file costs, that's a KOTC episode with your name on it.

The catch: one product, unnamed customers and no independent test data. PrimeSwitch is a damned clever relay, but it is a relay. "Any machine from code" has so far been shown by a single device family. I'd want to see a second, unrelated device before calling this a revolution rather than a very well-funded switch company.

By the numbers:

  • $250M raised to date. No valuation disclosed.
  • 50 microseconds for PrimeSwitch to open, "about a thousand times faster than a conventional contactor."
  • 150 amps continuous, with 1,500 volts of isolation.
  • 9.5 mm across and 3 mm thick. It holds its state at zero power.
  • Single-digit microns: the smallest features the Matter Compiler makes.

Sources: TNW · Business Wire release · Atomic Machines: PrimeSwitch · Holden's launch thread

Also on the wire

Podcast listeners have hit the ad ceiling

"A warning about podcast ad load?" Image: Podnews

The Podcast Study 2026 surveyed 1,205 US listeners aged 18 to 54. A majority say shows already carry too many ads, and about one in five say ads have made them listen less. Hal Rood of Strategic Solutions Research, who ran the study, called it "a flashing yellow light on ad load."

My read: this is good news in a bloody yellow raincoat, at least for a studio. If the easy money from adding spots is done, shows win with host-read ads and clips, and both start in a room with decent cameras and light. Social media is how most listeners find new shows. That's the Hollywood Podcasts sales pitch, written for you by a stranger. The catch: one survey, no published margin of error, and its authors sell a follow-up webinar.

  • 54.8% say podcasts already have too many ads. Only 8.6% would take more to keep shows free.
  • 53.2% find new shows through social media, against 17.4% through ads in other podcasts.

Sources: Podnews · Press release: The Podcast Study 2026
