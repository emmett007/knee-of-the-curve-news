The big one: why the body stops taking out its zombie cells

Nature Aging's diagram of how one of the cell's recycling systems and senescence collide with age. Image: Nature Aging

As we age, some cells stop dividing but refuse to die. Scientists call them senescent. Everyone else calls them zombie cells. They leak inflammatory signals into the tissue around them. A healthy immune system clears them out, but with age it stops keeping up. Two pieces of work this week explain part of the reason.

The first comes from Cedars-Sinai, in the journal Cell Metabolism. James Kirkland's team found that a protein called PD-L2 helps aging cells hide from the immune system. In mice, removing or blocking PD-L2 cut the build-up of senescent cells and the inflammatory signals they release. The mice also handled sugar better and had a stronger grip. In human cells, senescent ones made more PD-L2 than young ones, and levels rose with age in some human tissues. The team thinks PD-L2 could also work as a blood marker for how many zombie cells you are carrying.

The second landed in Nature Aging this morning. A commentary on new work by Sereda and colleagues says that one of the cell's recycling systems, called chaperone-mediated autophagy, declines with age. That decline leads to more senescent cells. Part of the cause is that macrophages, the immune system's clean-up cells, stop patrolling and clearing them properly.

My read This is a strong LSN story because it changes the question. The last decade asked how to kill zombie cells with drugs. This asks why the body stopped seeing them, and how to make it look again. One piece of context helps the audience: PD-L2 belongs to the same family of "don't attack me" signals that the checkpoint cancer drugs block, so the idea already has a famous cousin.

The catch It is mice, with no human trial yet. The authors have money on the outcome. The first author holds patents on PD-L2, some licensed to Rejuveron Senescence Therapeutics, and several authors hold senolytic patents through Mayo Clinic. I read the Nature Aging commentary, not the full Sereda paper behind it. Say "a protein that helps zombie cells hide, in mice." Don't say "a cure for aging."

Sources: ScienceDaily (Cedars-Sinai release) · Nature Aging commentary, Oct 19

Also on the wire: the mystery model called Space Bunny was MiniMax all along

MarkTechPost covered the launch this morning. Image: MarkTechPost

For weeks, an anonymous AI model called Space Bunny has been handling serious traffic. This morning the Chinese lab MiniMax owned up. It released MiniMax M3.1 Flash on its API and confirmed that Space Bunny was an earlier build of the same model. MarkTechPost reports that the stealth version processed more than 200 trillion tokens before anyone knew whose it was. Both "MiniMax M3" and "Space Bunny" are trending on X in the U.S. right now.

The selling point is price. M3.1 Flash costs $0.15 per million input tokens and $0.60 per million output tokens, half of MiniMax's own M3 rate per token. It reads text, images and video, holds a million tokens of context, and always reasons before answering, which cannot be switched off. MiniMax says it beats Opus 5 on an office-automation test, 62.2 to 50.3, but trails Opus 5 by 13.7 points on a hard coding test. The open weights are due Friday, Oct 23.

My read This is a KOTC story with a built-in punchline. Thousands of developers went on a blind date, liked it, and then found out who it was. The serious point under the joke is that agent work is getting cheap very fast, and the price war is now global.

The catch Every benchmark number is MiniMax's own, and nobody has replicated them yet. The licence for the weights hasn't been published. My assumption, not anything reported, is that sending business data through a Chinese API will make your audience nervous, so say so on air. And under the Captain Zero rule, none of your bots changes models without asking you first.

Short take: the afternoon nod-off may be a warning light

Image: Neuroscience News

Johns Hopkins followed 995 Medicare beneficiaries aged 65 and older for up to 10 years. Those who napped most days or every day had a 48% higher risk of dementia than those who rarely napped, after adjusting for night-time sleep and health. Falling asleep without meaning to carried a 39% higher risk. Short naps of 30 minutes or less carried a 40% higher risk. The senior author, Adam Spira, says frequent napping may be an early sign of brain changes rather than their cause. My read: this is a Longevity Latte morning-routine angle, because the lever your audience controls is how rested they are when the day starts. That is my framing, not the study's. The catch: it is observational and self-reported, so it shows a link, not proof.

Sources: Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School · Neuroscience News

Short take: half a cup beat six

Image: Health Chosun (Clipart Korea)

A study in Scientific Reports followed 453,913 UK Biobank participants for an average of 15.1 years. Among people with high blood pressure, those who drank half a cup to one cup of coffee a day had about a 27% lower risk of dementia than those who drank six or more. Ground coffee drinkers had about a 32% lower risk than decaf drinkers. Four to five cups of tea a day showed the lowest risk of all. The study counted a cup as 75 mg of caffeine, roughly half of a tall Americano. My read: the message for Longevity Latte is moderation over megadoses, which suits a morning cup built on more than caffeine. The catch: it is observational, and the comparison group is very heavy drinkers, so don't sell it as "coffee prevents dementia."

Source: Health Chosun

Podcasting this week: candidates want your microphone, and Apple's video-ad toll slips

Image: Podnews Daily via Podfollow

New Sounds Profitable data out today says one in four U.S. podcast listeners say a candidate's podcast appearance might influence their vote, with the midterms two weeks away. Listening is split almost evenly by party, though Edison Research finds independents spend the most time with podcasts. Apple Podcasts plans to charge ad networks a per-impression fee for its new dynamic audio-and-video ads, charged whether people watch or only listen; Podnews understands that fee has slipped to early 2027. Acast has put a show into Apple's video format for the first time. Australia's PodPoll 26 found 60% of podcast consumers prefer to listen and 23% prefer to watch. Spots Now also launched custom AI agents for podcast networks.

My read The midterm number is a Hollywood Podcasts sales angle this week. Candidates and campaigns in LA need a room that looks good on camera at short notice, and you sell exactly that. The audio-first number from Australia is a useful reminder that clean sound still matters as much as the cameras. The catch Both figures are self-reported survey answers, and the Apple fee lands on ad networks, not on studios.
