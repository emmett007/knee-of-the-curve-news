The big one: the man building AGI just moved his own deadline up again

Image via Startup Fortune

Demis Hassabis now expects artificial general intelligence within two to three years. He said so in a long Wall Street Journal Magazine profile by Ben Cohen, published on Wednesday as the magazine's technology innovator cover. Earlier this year he put it at three to five years. In January at Davos, according to Startup Fortune's timeline, he was saying five to ten.

His definition hasn't changed: a system with the full cognitive range of a human brain, not just some of it. What has changed is how close he thinks we are. "It's soon," he told the Journal. "I'm updating nearer and nearer." He describes winning as "helping humanity steward AGI into the world safely and securely for the benefit of everyone." Startup Fortune also points out that his number now matches the two-to-three-year window Anthropic's Dario Amodei gave in January 2025.

My read This is a gift for KOTC. A deadline that keeps getting closer every time someone checks it is a comedy premise on its own. The useful point for your audience: the people closest to the work are shortening their forecasts, not stretching them. Hassabis has also admitted, per Startup Fortune, that he picks blunt language on purpose to make governments move faster. So the forecast is partly a message.

The catch I read the Journal piece through Mint's reprint, not on wsj.com, which is paywalled. Sources disagree on his "Einstein test." X's summary says it means rebuilding Einstein's 1905 papers from pre-1901 knowledge. Startup Fortune says it means deriving general relativity from knowledge up to 1911. Don't quote the test's details on air until you've checked the original. And no system has passed it.

Sources: WSJ profile, reprinted by Mint, Oct 21 · Startup Fortune, Oct 22 · X story: Hassabis shortens AGI timeline

For LSN: your face and your heartbeat are both telling on you

Where the AI looks: heat maps of the face regions FAHR-Face relies on. Image via Scientific American

Two studies landed this week with the same idea. Ordinary medical data already holds warnings that doctors can't see.

The first is FAHR-Face, from Mass General Brigham, published Monday in The Lancet Digital Health. It's an AI trained on more than 40 million face photos, then tuned for two jobs. One version estimates biological age from a photo, with an average error of 5.1 years on public test sets. The other predicts survival in cancer patients and was tuned on 34,363 patient photos. People in its highest-risk quarter had more than three times the death rate of the lowest quarter, even after adjusting for other clinical factors. In one test group, 64 of the 122 highest-risk patients died within 90 days of the photo, according to Scientific American. The result held up in a separate group of nearly 5,000 patients in the Netherlands.

Image via SciTechDaily

The second comes from UC Berkeley, in Nature. Ziad Obermeyer's team trained an AI on more than 440,000 routine EKGs from Sweden, linked to death records. It then checked the model on patients in San Diego and Taipei. The AI found a group with a 7% yearly rate of sudden cardiac death. The standard screen, which measures how much blood the heart pumps per beat, found a group with 4.6%. Many people the AI flagged would not have counted as high risk at all. More than 300,000 Americans die of sudden cardiac arrest each year. And about two-thirds of implanted defibrillators never have to fire, which shows how poorly current screening picks patients.

My read The LSN angle is "cheap signals, early warning." A selfie and a ten-second EKG are about the cheapest health data there is. Both studies say they hide information about how fast someone is ageing or how much danger they're in.

The catch Neither tool is ready for the clinic. FAHR-Face was trained mostly on white Boston patients. An outside expert, Surrey's Miroslaw Bober, found that a smile or a change of lighting moved its age estimate by about two years. Mass General Brigham also has a patent pending. The EKG model can't explain what it's detecting, and nobody has shown yet that using it saves lives. The Nature paper is dated June 24. Only Berkeley's release is new this week. Say "spots risk." Don't say "predicts your death."

Sources: The Lancet Digital Health, Oct 19 · Scientific American · Science Media Centre expert reaction, Oct 19 · SciTechDaily (UC Berkeley release), Oct 20 · X story: AI predicts cancer survival from face photos

For Hollywood Podcasts: The Ringer is a video podcast company now, and its writers are leaving for Substack

Sean Fennessey. Image: Erika Goldring/Getty Images, via Defector

Defector published a sharp piece yesterday on The Ringer, the Spotify-owned sports and culture brand. Four of its best-known names, Sean Fennessey, Zach Lowe, Tom Pelissero and Bryan Curtis, have launched newsletters on Substack, not on The Ringer's own site. Only Curtis's is free. Lowe charges $10 a month, Pelissero $8 and Fennessey $5. Lowe's newsletter passed 24,000 subscribers in its first week. Defector's verdict on the parent company is blunt: "These days, The Ringer is primarily a video podcast company." Nobody at The Ringer, Spotify or Substack would explain the arrangement.

My read Two trends meet here, and both help the studio pitch. First, the biggest names in sports media earn their living on camera, talking. Second, the people with audiences are going direct. A host with their own following needs exactly what Hollywood Podcasts sells: a professional room, cameras and a crew, without signing on with a network.

The catch This is one outlet's reading of a company that wouldn't comment. Treat "video podcast company" as Defector's description, not The Ringer's.
