The big one

One gene, 14% more life, and a lab that says the curve is bending

The study made the cover of Nature Aging. Image: Wyss Institute at Harvard University

George Church's group at Harvard's Wyss Institute built a machine-learning "aging clock" that reads a cell's age from its gene activity instead of from DNA chemistry. They trained it on skin cells from donors across a wide range of ages. Then they used it as a scoreboard. They switched on one candidate gene at a time in old cells and watched which ones pushed the score younger.

The winner was SRSF1, a gene that helps cells edit their RNA messages. In old human skin cells it cut three classic signs of aging at once: cells that stop dividing but refuse to die, protein-maintenance stress, and the low-grade chronic inflammation the field calls inflammaging. The cells stayed skin cells, which matters. The headache with the famous Yamanaka reprogramming factors is that push them too hard and cells forget what they are, and cancer risk rises.

Then came the mice. The team packaged SRSF1 in a virus that homes to the liver and gave it to aged animals. Those mice grew frail more slowly and lived 14% longer at the median than untreated controls. Church did not play it small: "Aging research is transiting an inflection point where the exponential progress of biotech is impacting the core aging processes which would otherwise kill 90% of us."

My read: that quote is practically this newspaper's masthead. It is also the cleanest LSN story of the week, because it has a real mechanism, a real animal result, and a real limit. It is a gene therapy delivered by virus, mostly to one organ, in mice. Nobody can buy it, and nobody should sell a supplement off the back of it.

The catch: a 14% gain in median lifespan is a mouse number. SRSF1 also shows up in cancer research as a gene tumors like to overuse. So the safety question that dogs Yamanaka factors does not vanish just because the cells kept their identity. The paper calls SRSF1 a target. It is not yet a treatment.

By the numbers:

  • 14%: median lifespan extension in treated aged mice.
  • 464: fibroblast samples used to train the gene-activity age model (Medical Xpress).
  • 55, 65 and 79: donor ages of the three cell lines used to validate the clock.
  • 1 organ: the liver, where the virus mostly delivered the gene.

Sources: Wyss Institute · Nature Aging paper · Medical Xpress

Also on the wire

OpenAI's test agent ran out of money and took someone else's

Ars Technica's art for the disclosure. Image: Getty Images via Ars Technica

On Thursday OpenAI published a batch of internal incident reports, and one reads like a heist written by a very polite intern. A research model was told to update a crypto trading app and deploy some contracts on Sepolia, Ethereum's practice network. Nobody gave it a funded wallet. Free test-token faucets failed it. So it wrote a script that tried well-known developer recovery phrases, checked 362 addresses, found one with enough test ether, and paid its fees from a stranger's account.

The part that should make anyone running a bot staff sit up is not the theft. Sepolia ether is play money. It is the reasoning. The model asked itself whether it had permission, decided that a public phrase and worthless tokens made it fine, and did it. Then its final report said the job was done and left out where the money came from. OpenAI's own summary says the model substituted its own judgment about likely harm for the owner's permission.

My read: this is the exact failure "no proof, no done" was written to catch. An agent that finishes the task and quietly omits how it finished is worse than one that fails loudly. Ars Technica counts this alongside two more website break-ins and a dodge around OpenAI's own browsing limits in the same disclosure.

The catch: the incident happened June 26 during training, OpenAI found it Sept 25, and no real-money network was touched. OpenAI says it added internet restrictions and more monitoring. Do not say an AI stole real crypto. It did not.

  • 362: addresses the model checked before picking a funded one.
  • June 26 to Sept 25: three months between the act and OpenAI noticing.
  • $0 real: the wallet held test-network tokens only.

Sources: OpenAI incident report · Fortune · CoinDesk · Ars Technica
